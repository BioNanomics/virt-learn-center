# Research brief: IU Fort Wayne immersive health professions laboratory

Prepared September 10, 2026. Read [the cleaned source context](IU_Immersive_Learning_Context.txt) first. This brief prepares future research; no external verification has been completed.

## Decision to support

Determine a practical, fundable way to launch and sustain a shared laboratory in IU's already leased NIIC space that helps students master foundational science and apply it across nursing, health sciences, dental hygiene, and medical imaging. The proposal should include VR, appropriate educational AI, accessible alternatives, and no required student purchases for lab platforms or materials.

Produce a recommendation suitable for IU leadership and grant development, with a phased scope, defensible costs, staffing coverage, implementation dependencies, and an evaluation plan. Define BioNanomics' intended contribution before assigning it deliverables or a formal partnership role.

## Starting evidence and constraints

- **Reported locally:** existing leased NIIC space; approximately $10,000 for connectivity; minor renovation needed; retention and difficult science prerequisites are central concerns.
- **Unconfirmed planning assumptions:** 2,000–3,000 square feet, a roughly 24-student teaching group, 8–12 headsets, six workstations, and the proposed room zones.
- **Unverified external material:** all vendor features, licenses, price examples, research findings, and institutional precedents in the copied ChatGPT text.
- **Budget issues already found:** startup rows sum to $195,000, or $197,500 if the $10,000 local connectivity figure replaces the original $7,500 allowance for the same scope. Annual operating rows sum to $62,500–$82,500. Startup and annual software may overlap.
- **Access limitation:** the original conversation behind IU authentication and its claimed citations were not available. Use the supplied text as context, then locate independent primary sources.

## Local discovery questions

These questions can guide a site visit or planning meeting. They are not prerequisites to desk research; carry unknowns as explicit assumptions.

1. What are the usable dimensions, occupancy limit, current electrical and network capacity, accessibility constraints, and renovation permissions? Does the $10,000 connectivity estimate include Wi-Fi, cabling, installation, security configuration, and recurring service?
2. What grant or funding opportunity is being considered? Establish sponsor, deadline, funding ceiling, allowable capital/personnel/software expenses, indirect-cost rules, required match, and support after the grant.
3. Which courses and cohorts have the most urgent learning or progression needs? Obtain aggregate enrollment, course success, repeat/withdrawal patterns, retention definitions, and current material costs.
4. What headsets, simulation equipment, anatomical models, imaging software, educational AI licenses, rooms, and support staff does IU already have? Which can be shared at the NIIC?
5. Who owns the project academically, operationally, financially, and technically? Identify faculty leads from all four professional programs and foundational sciences, plus IT, accessibility, facilities, procurement, and assessment contacts.
6. What staffed teaching and open-lab hours are required? How many simultaneous users, sections, and faculty sessions must be supported? Is off-campus access part of the requirement?
7. What would BioNanomics contribute: research, technology evaluation, grant development, software, implementation, or another role? Record the answer without assuming a formal relationship.

## Research workstreams

### 1. Educational need and evidence

Find evidence that distinguishes spatial anatomy learning, knowledge retention, clinical reasoning, practical skill transfer, and student retention. Assess study design, population, comparator, effect size, uncertainty, accessibility, and implementation burden. Do not infer improved retention from satisfaction scores or short-term quiz gains.

Resolve the source chat's unnamed 2026 meta-analysis and IU medical-imaging precedent. Include systematic reviews and relevant primary studies, with enough original research to understand how the interventions were delivered. Compare VR with lower-cost 3D desktop, physical models, and conventional teaching where evidence permits.

Identify a small initial set of faculty-owned modules with a specific learning problem, objective, activity, assessment, staff time, and expected learner throughput. Include a useful launch experience for each named program and foundational sciences. Treat the suggested 10–15 modules as a scope option to cost.

### 2. Platform and content selection

Assess the named candidates: 3D Organon, Complete Anatomy, BioDigital Human, ZygoteBody, OpenStax, Crash Course, BodyParts3D/Anatomography, and the claimed Elsevier VR simulation offering. Add alternatives only when they fill a documented requirement.

For each viable candidate record:

- Instructional fit by course/program, content quality, faculty authoring, and assessment features.
- Headset/desktop support, shared displays, concurrent sessions, classroom management, and network needs.
- Institutional terms, shared-device rights, guest-session limits, student access duration, identity integration, and accessibility.
- Export and interoperability, data collected, hosting, support, implementation effort, and annual renewal conditions.
- Quote status, price basis, seat/device/concurrency assumptions, and full three-year cost.

Verify official linking, embedding, downloading, adaptation, attribution, and distribution rights for the actual resources and use cases. Distinguish open content, free individual access, institution-funded access, and time-limited trials. Route unresolved legal interpretations to the appropriate IU office.

### 3. Space and technology design

Develop a layout based on measured space and approved capacity. Compare a shared-display teaching room with rotating VR stations against projection and more extensive simulation options. Include a seated/non-headset route to the same learning objectives.

Specify quantities and functional requirements for displays, headsets, charging/storage, cleaning, workstations, audiovisual controls, device management, and connectivity. Establish which tasks actually require local GPU workstations; do not assume every AI activity requires a local “AI PC.”

Determine educational display needs separately from clinical diagnostic requirements. Inventory existing nursing, dental, imaging, and ultrasound resources before recommending duplicate purchases. Price renovation, installation, training, warranties, taxes/shipping where applicable, and replacement cycles separately.

### 4. Educational AI

Select a small set of measurable pilot activities, such as patient interviewing, handoff practice, and physiology explanations, using an IU-approved environment. Define faculty review, case grounding, feedback rubrics, model-error handling, and the boundary between formative feedback and competency decisions.

Use fictional or appropriately governed teaching data. Verify current IU requirements for privacy, accessibility, retention, research use, and student access. Estimate costs from sessions, usage limits, voice/text needs, integrations, and support. Identify whether existing institutional access covers the proposed use.

### 5. Operations and staffing

Compare a one-position launch supported by explicitly allocated existing faculty/IT time with the two-position coordinator-plus-specialist model in the source. Obtain local classification and salary/benefit guidance rather than treating the pasted ranges as salary evidence.

Model teaching support, setup/reset, maintenance, faculty development, content preparation, open-lab coverage, and absence coverage. Price student assistants from wage, hours per week, and weeks per year; test whether the $10,000 allowance can support the suggested 4–6 assistants. Identify what service scope a lean staffing model cannot support.

### 6. Funding and sustainable budget

Build three comparable scenarios: a minimum viable pilot, a supported interdisciplinary launch, and a later enhanced center. Use the same coverage period and cost definitions across scenarios. Do not force estimates to match the prior $200,000 or $425,000 figures.

Separate:

1. One-time equipment, space preparation, installation, and initial training.
2. Year-one recurring software, support, staffing, supplies, and facility expenses.
3. Years two and three renewals, staffing, maintenance, and replacement reserve.
4. Existing institutional contributions, requested cash funding, contingency, and unresolved scope.

Each budget line should carry quantity, unit cost, basis/source, date, one-time or recurring status, first-year coverage, and confidence. Make overlap checks explicit for software, connectivity, installation, and student staffing. Present a three-year total cost of ownership and sensitivity to enrollment, concurrent use, license terms, and staff coverage.

Research plausible grants and healthcare/philanthropic partnerships, checking current eligibility, deadlines, award ranges, and allowed expenses at official sources. Distinguish opportunities from commitments. Prepare outreach drafts for review; do not contact partners or vendors without explicit authorization to send messages.

### 7. Evaluation and implementation

Define success before procurement. Use course-level learning measures and program progression/retention outcomes, supported by faculty workload, access, utilization, and cost data. State denominators, time windows, baselines, comparison strategy, and limitations. Assess uneven access and the experience of students who cannot use headsets.

Create a phased schedule with decision points for facilities readiness, software/hardware demonstrations, faculty acceptance, staff training, pilot review, and expansion. Reconcile launch AI pilots with the source's Year 2 AI expansion language. Identify required institutional approvals and likely procurement lead times from current evidence.
